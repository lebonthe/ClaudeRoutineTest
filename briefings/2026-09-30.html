4) Zhuangzi — Essay 12: 天地 (Tiān Dì) — "Heaven and Earth"

The fifth chapter of the Outer Chapters (外篇), following "Letting Be and Leaving Alone." It opens by claiming that Heaven and Earth are vast yet share one transformation, the myriad things differ yet share one order, and the ruler should govern by inaction: "The Dao is what covers and sustains all things" (夫道,覆載萬物者也), so one who "acts through non-action is called Heavenly" (無為為之之謂天). The chapter's most famous story is the "machine heart" (機心) parable: Zigong meets an old gardener who hauls water in a jar rather than using a well-sweep, and says that whoever uses machines develops a machine-like mind, which disturbs the spirit and blocks the Dao. Zigong returns shaken; Confucius explains that the gardener is a follower of "the arts of Hun Dun (Chaos)." Another set piece, Yellow Emperor's lost pearl (玄珠), is found not by Knowledge, Keen Sight or Eloquence, but by "Xiang Wang" (象罔, "Form-Without-Form"). Themes: the primacy of naturalness over technique, the uses and limits of cleverness, and the Daoist sage as one who "forgets" both things and Heaven. Read it as a counterweight to today's Android verification and AI-model news: how much of our tooling do we adopt without asking what it does to the mind using it?

5) Country / Region Spotlight — Tunisia (突尼西亞)

  • Location & territory size: Tunisia is the northernmost country in Africa, on the Mediterranean coast between Algeria to the west and Libya to the southeast; its Cape Angela is Africa's northernmost point. At about 163,600 km², it is roughly 4.5 times the size of Taiwan (about 36,200 km²) and slightly smaller than the US state of Florida and about two-thirds the size of the United Kingdom. The Sahara covers much of the south; the north is fertile Mediterranean coast.
  • Population: About 12 million (the 2024 census counted roughly 11.97 million), concentrated along the coast; the Tunis metropolitan area holds a large share.
  • Religion: Overwhelmingly Muslim (well over 95%, mostly Sunni, of the Maliki school), with very small Christian and Jewish communities; the Jewish community on the island of Djerba is among the oldest in the Arab world and the site of the ancient El Ghriba synagogue.
  • Language: Arabic is official; Tunisian Arabic (Derja) is the everyday spoken form and French remains widely used in business, education and media. A small Amazigh (Berber) minority speaks Tamazight.
  • Economy: A lower-middle-income economy of roughly US$50 billion in nominal GDP, built on tourism, olive oil (Tunisia is among the world's top exporters), phosphates, textiles and automotive wiring/electronics manufacturing for European firms. It has struggled with high public debt, inflation, unemployment (especially among young graduates) and subsidy pressures, and has long negotiated with the IMF.
  • History: Phoenician settlers founded Carthage traditionally around 814 BC; it became a Mediterranean power that fought Rome in the Punic Wars until its destruction in 146 BC (Hannibal is its most famous general). The region became Roman Africa, was ruled by Vandals and Byzantines, and was conquered by Arab armies in the 7th century; Kairouan (founded 670) became a major center of Islam. It passed through the Aghlabid, Fatimid, Hafsid dynasties, then Ottoman rule from 1574, and became a French protectorate in 1881. Independence came on March 20, 1956 under Habib Bourguiba, who ruled until 1987, followed by Zine El Abidine Ben Ali until 2011. The "Jasmine Revolution," triggered by the self-immolation of vendor Mohamed Bouazizi in December 2010, toppled Ben Ali and sparked the Arab Spring. A democratic constitution followed in 2014, but in July 2021 President Kais Saied suspended parliament and later rewrote the constitution (2022).
  • Current leader: President Kais Saied, a former constitutional-law professor elected in 2019 and re-elected in October 2024 with roughly 90% of the vote on low turnout; capital Tunis; currency the Tunisian dinar. Prime ministers change frequently, so check current office holders.
  • Fun fact: Parts of Star Wars were filmed in Tunisia (Tatooine scenes near Tozeur and Matmata's underground homes), and the country's name "Tatooine" comes from the Tunisian town of Tataouine.

6) Internet Meme Spotlight — "Leeroy Jenkins"

  • Name: "LEEEEROOOOY JENKINS!" — a battle cry synonymous with charging into something recklessly without a plan.
  • Origin / history: In spring 2005 a video circulated of a group of World of Warcraft players in the guild "Pals for Life" preparing to raid Upper Blackrock Spire. While the group carefully discusses a detailed strategy with percentages and a calculator, one player, Leeroy, who had stepped away from the keyboard, returns and bellows his name while charging in alone, dragging everyone into a wipe. Trying to save the plan, his teammates lament, and Leeroy's only defense is "At least I have chicken" (referring to the meal he'd been eating). The player behind Leeroy, Ben Schulz, later acknowledged the video was staged, though it was widely believed real at first.
  • How it became popular: It spread across gaming forums, early YouTube, and mainstream press in 2005-2006, helped by a TV segment and by Blizzard adding nods to the character in the game itself.
  • Meaning and usage: Today, "pulling a Leeroy Jenkins" means abandoning the plan and jumping in recklessly, often with comedic or disastrous results — used for gaming, sports, and office or startup decisions.
  • Variations / evolution: The shout is used as an audio meme, in parodies, and as a verb ("Leeroying it"). Blizzard turned it into a legendary Hearthstone card, "Leeroy Jenkins" (a 5-mana charge minion that gives the opponent two whelps).
  • Current status: Still a durable gaming-culture shorthand for reckless action, occasionally revived in new games and memes.

7) Film / Performing-Arts Spotlight — Hamilton (musical, 2015, Lin-Manuel Miranda)

  • Name and key figures: Hamilton: An American Musical, with book, music and lyrics by Lin-Manuel Miranda, who also originated the title role. Directed by Thomas Kail, choreographed by Andy Blankenbuehler, with music direction and orchestrations by Alex Lacamoire. Original cast included Leslie Odom Jr. (Aaron Burr), Phillipa Soo (Eliza Hamilton), Renée Elise Goldsberry (Angelica Schuyler), Daveed Diggs (Lafayette / Jefferson), Christopher Jackson (George Washington) and Jonathan Groff (King George III).
  • Era / background: Miranda began writing after reading Ron Chernow's 2004 biography Alexander Hamilton on vacation. It premiered Off-Broadway at The Public Theater in early 2015 and moved to Broadway's Richard Rodgers Theatre in August 2015.
  • Content overview: A sung-through story of Alexander Hamilton, the immigrant from the Caribbean who became George Washington's aide, the first Treasury Secretary, and the architect of the American financial system, tracing his rise, his rivalry with Aaron Burr, his marriage to Eliza Schuyler, a scandalous affair, the loss of his son Philip, and his death in a duel with Burr in 1804. The show ends by asking "Who lives, who dies, who tells your story?"
  • Artistic style and techniques: A score blending hip-hop, R&B, pop, and classic Broadway; dense internal rhyme and rapid-fire lyrics; recurring musical leitmotifs; and deliberate casting of actors of color as the Founding Fathers to reclaim the story as "America then, told by America now." Blankenbuehler's choreography uses a revolving turntable in the stage floor and ensemble movement that rewinds and freezes moments.
  • Cultural / historical significance: It reshaped who is seen in the founding narrative, renewed public interest in Hamilton (including the retention of him on the US $10 bill), and brought hip-hop storytelling to the center of American theatre.
  • Influence and legacy: 16 Tony nominations and 11 Tony wins in 2016 (including Best Musical), the 2016 Pulitzer Prize for Drama, and a Grammy for its cast album. Its success set a template for genre-crossing musicals and drove a surge of diverse casting.
  • Current status / where to experience it: Filmed from the original cast and released on Disney+ in July 2020. Stage productions have run on Broadway, in London's West End, and on North American tours; check current listings for running productions.

Sources: general performing-arts reference knowledge (Tony Awards / Pulitzer records); no live source consulted, so verify current productions and streaming availability locally.

8) Film-Appreciation Method — Want vs. Need (Character Arc) (Lesson 62)

Earlier lessons taught how to read images, sound, cutting, and dialogue subtext; this one teaches how to read a character's engine. Most well-built protagonists carry two things: a want (the conscious, external goal they pursue in the plot) and a need (the internal lesson or truth they must face to change, often one they resist). A character arc is the distance between the two. To analyze it, ask: (1) What does the character say they want, and what do they do to pursue it? (2) What flaw, wound, or belief blocks them? (3) What do they really need, and which scene forces them to choose between want and need? (4) Does the ending give them the want, the need, both, or neither, and what is the film saying by that choice? The most revealing moment is usually the decision scene where want and need collide.

Worked example: Casablanca (1942, dir. Michael Curtiz). Rick Blaine's want is stated in the famous line "I stick my neck out for nobody": neutrality, staying in his café and out of politics and love. His need is to recover his faith and his willingness to commit to something beyond himself. The airport scene is the collision: Ilsa is at his mercy, the letters of transit are in his hand, and everything he wanted (to keep her, to stay out of it) is on offer. Watch how the film stages the choice: fog closing the frame, the propellers, the tight two-shots between Rick and Ilsa, and Rick's calm, almost clipped delivery as he tells her she'd "regret it — maybe not today, maybe not tomorrow, but soon and for the rest of your life." He gives up the want (Ilsa) to honor the need (the cause). Then Renault's line "Round up the usual suspects" and the closing walk show his need was met. It works because the film has set up the want early, repeats it, and only lets Rick resolve it when it costs him what he most desired.

  • 起源/歷史:2005 年春天流傳一段影片:《魔獸世界》公會「Pals for Life」的玩家準備攻打黑石塔上層。當隊伍正用百分比與計算機認真討論詳細戰術時,離開鍵盤的玩家李洛伊回來了,一邊大喊自己的名字一邊獨自衝進去,拖著全隊團滅。隊友哀嚎之際,李洛伊唯一的辯解是「至少我有雞肉」(指他當時正在吃的餐點)。李洛伊背後的玩家班ㆍ舒爾茨(Ben Schulz)後來承認影片是事先安排的,但起初大家普遍以為是真的。
  • 如何流行:2005–2006 年間透過遊戲論壇、早期 YouTube 與主流媒體擴散,加上電視節目報導,以及暴雪在遊戲中加入致敬彩蛋。
  • 意義與使用情境:今天「pull a Leeroy Jenkins」指拋開計畫、莽撞跳進去,結果常常是喜劇或災難——用於遊戲、運動,也用於職場或創業決策。
  • 變體/演變:這句吼聲被當作音訊迷因、惡搞影片,也被當動詞使用(「Leeroying it」)。暴雪把它做成《爐石戰記》傳說卡「Leeroy Jenkins」(5 費衝鋒隨從,並給對手兩隻雛龍)。
  • 現況:至今仍是遊戲文化中「魯莽行動」的通用簡稱,偶爾在新遊戲與新迷因中復活。

8)看片方法——「想要」與「需要」(角色弧線)(第 62 課)

前面的課程教過如何讀影像、聲音、剪接與對白潛台詞;這一課教如何讀角色的「引擎」。多數結構紮實的主角身上帶著兩樣東西:想要(want)——他們在劇情中追逐的、有意識的外在目標;以及需要(need)——他們必須面對、才能真正改變的內在真相,往往是他們抗拒的。角色弧線,就是兩者之間的距離。分析時問四個問題:(1)角色說自己想要什麼?為此做了什麼?(2)什麼缺陷、創傷或信念擋住了他?(3)他真正需要的是什麼?哪一場戲逼他在「想要」與「需要」之間做選擇?(4)結局給了他想要的、需要的、兩者,或兩者皆無?影片透過這個選擇想說什麼?最有啟發性的時刻通常是「想要」與「需要」正面相撞的決定場景。

示範:《北非諜影》(Casablanca,1942,導演:麥可·柯蒂茲)。瑞克·布連的「想要」在他那句名言中說得很清楚:「我不會為任何人冒險」——保持中立,待在他的咖啡館,遠離政治與愛情。他的「需要」是找回信念,並願意投身超越自己的事。機場那場戲就是兩者相撞的時刻:伊爾莎任他擺布,通行證握在他手上,他所想要的一切(留住她、置身事外)都擺在眼前。看影片如何調度這個選擇:霧氣封住畫面、螺旋槳、瑞克與伊爾莎之間的緊湊雙人鏡頭,以及瑞克冷靜近乎簡短的口吻告訴她:「妳會後悔的——也許不是今天、也許不是明天,但很快,而且是一輩子。」他放棄了「想要」(伊爾莎),成全了「需要」(那個志業)。接著雷諾那句「把常見的嫌疑犯都抓起來」與結尾的並肩離去,證明他的需要已被滿足。這場戲之所以成立,是因為影片早早建立並反覆強調「想要」,並且只有在解決它要付出他最渴望之物的代價時,才讓瑞克解決它。
